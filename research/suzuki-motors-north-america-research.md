## Walking the Factory Floor - Rome, GA

Here is a station-by-station walkthrough of what you'd see if you walked through SMAC:

### Zone 1: Raw Materials Receiving & Pipe Storage
You enter through the receiving area. Raw steel pipe in various diameters is stored on racks. This is the starting material for every ATV frame. Just-In-Time delivery means regional suppliers are constantly dropping off materials.

### Zone 2: Pipe Bending - Hydraulic Press Area
Straight pipes are fed into **hydraulic presses** that bend them into the specific shapes needed for frame sections. The press area is loud, industrial. Multiple press stations handle different pipe diameters and bend angles for different frame sections (front, rear, top, bottom rails). These are large, floor-mounted hydraulic press brakes.

### Zone 3: Robotic Plasma Cutting
After bending, pipes move to the **robotic plasma cutting cells**. Robot arms equipped with plasma torches cut the bent pipes to precise lengths and profiles. This is enclosed behind safety barriers with viewing windows. You'd see bright arc light, sparks, and the characteristic hiss of plasma cutting. The robots are 6-axis industrial arms (likely Yaskawa Motoman or similar Japanese OEM - see robot section below).

### Zone 4: Welding - The Heart of Fabrication
This is the largest and most complex zone. Two types of welding happen here:

**Robotic Welding Cells:**
- Multiple enclosed robotic arc welding cells
- Robots perform MIG/MAG arc welding to join frame sub-assemblies
- Small pipe pieces ("kiddie parts") are welded into larger "mother parts"
- The left, right, top, and bottom sections of each frame are built in sub-assembly cells
- Fixtures and positioners hold the parts while robot arms weld at high speed
- Orange/yellow sparks visible through safety glass. Loud buzzing of arc welding.

**Manual Welding Stations:**
- Human welders with auto-darkening welding helmets handle the tight-space welds where robot arms physically can't fit
- Workers wear full protective gear; the mask window is light-sensitive and auto-darkens when welding starts
- These stations are interspersed between robotic cells

Frame sub-assemblies converge and are welded into complete ATV frames. An ATV frame takes approximately 6 hours from raw pipe to finished welded frame.

### Zone 5: E-Coat (Electro-Coating) Treatment
Completed frames go through an automated electro-coating system:
- Frames are first stripped/cleaned to remove impurities
- Then dipped in an electro-coat bath that provides corrosion-resistant coating
- "They're painted black; everything is black"
- The e-coat is specifically noted for standing up to harsh conditions like salt water (beach patrol ATVs)
- This is an automated conveyor system - frames hang from overhead conveyors and dip through treatment tanks

### Zone 6: Powder-Coat Paint Line
After e-coat, frames move to the **powder-coat paint system**:
- Electrostatic powder application
- Baking/curing ovens
- This was added as an expansion after the plant's initial opening
- Provides the final durable finish coat over the e-coat base

### Zone 7: Fender Manufacturing - Injection Molding
Separate from the frame line, fenders are made in-house:
- A large **injection-molding machine** pulls small resin granules through a hose
- Resin is melted and molded using **3,300 tons of pressure**
- Red and black fenders are produced on-site
- Some black fenders are sent to another facility for camouflage coating
- After molding, workers trim the fenders and apply protective coating while they cure
- Stickers and logos are applied at a separate station
- Different fender shapes require different molds (mold changeovers for model variants)

### Zone 8: Engine Assembly
Added in **2014** as a major expansion. Before this, engines were assembled in Japan.
- Sub-assembly of complete engines from components
- This was "the final step in assuring Suzuki engines were assembled 100 percent in the United States"
- Detailed process not publicly documented, but includes crankshaft, cylinder, transmission, and complete drivetrain assembly

### Zone 9: Pre-Assembly
Before the main line, sub-assemblies are prepared:
- Handlebars assembled
- Wiring harnesses prepared
- Other component sub-assemblies built
- These feed into the main assembly line as kits

### Zone 10: Main Assembly Line
The core production line where everything comes together:
- **Adjustable-height floor** - the assembly line floor can be raised or lowered to fit the particular model being built, so workers can access different areas of the vehicle
- Workers use **pneumatic and battery-powered tools**
- Sequential stations where components are mounted to the frame:
  - Engine mounting
  - Shock absorbers
  - Fenders
  - Tires
  - Electrical/wiring
  - Controls
- Each ATV-in-process spends ~89 seconds at each station (as of historical data; likely optimized since)
- At peak, the line can produce ~310 ATVs/day (~80,000/year capacity)
- Current actual output: ~60-120/day depending on demand

### Zone 11: Testing
Second-to-last station:
- A **seesaw-like device** lifts each ATV off the assembly-line pedestal and places it onto its wheels
- Workers add gasoline to the tank
- Each ATV is driven onto a **drum tester** (treadmill-like apparatus)
- Technician goes through a full checklist: lights, engine, steering, brakes, etc.
- Every single ATV is tested before shipping

### Zone 12: Packing & Shipping
Final station:
- ATVs are packaged for shipping
- Stored in on-site warehouse
- Shipped domestically (60%) and internationally (40%)

## Robots at SMAC: Types, Likely Brands & Partnerships

### Confirmed Robot Applications at SMAC

Based on all available public documentation, SMAC uses robots in three confirmed applications:

| Application | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Plasma Cutting** | 6-axis robotic arm with plasma torch | Cuts bent pipes to precise length/profile |
| **Arc Welding (MIG/MAG)** | 6-axis robotic arm in welding cells | Welds frame sub-assemblies ("kiddie parts" into "mother parts") |
| **Frame Sub-Assembly** | Robotic manipulation/positioning | Robotic subassembly of ATV frames |

Additionally, the painting/coating lines use automated conveyor systems (not articulated robots per se, but automated industrial systems).

### Most Likely Robot Brands (Analysis)

SMAC does not publicly disclose their robot suppliers. However, based on strong circumstantial evidence:

#### Primary Assessment: Yaskawa Motoman (High Probability)

**Evidence:**
1. **ADF Welding** - located in Rome, GA (same city as SMAC) - is a **Yaskawa Motoman Authorized Solution Provider (MSP)** and also services Panasonic/Miller industrial robots
2. ADF has been operating in NW Georgia for 20+ years (since 1990), overlapping SMAC's 2001 founding
3. ADF specializes in robotic integration, programming, maintenance, training, and repairs - exactly the services a plant like SMAC would need from a local partner
4. ADF programs Motoman, Panasonic, Fanuc, and OTC robots
5. SMAC emphasizes "Just-In-Time" delivery and **regional suppliers** - having your robotics integrator in the same city is textbook JIT
6. Yaskawa Motoman is Japanese (Yaskawa Electric Corporation, Kitakyushu, Japan) - aligning with Suzuki's preference for Japanese technology partners
7. The Yaskawa Motoman **ArcWorld** pre-engineered welding cells are the industry standard for exactly this type of tubular steel frame arc welding
8. A published case study shows Motoman robots welding motorcycle frames at 15-20 min/cycle with 56 welds per frame - directly analogous to ATV frame welding

**Most likely robot models at SMAC:**

| Model | Application | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Motoman AR1440** | Arc welding cells | 6-axis, 1440mm reach, 12kg payload, hollow-arm for torch cable routing. The go-to model for MIG arc welding of vehicle frames |
| **Motoman MA1900** | Arc welding / plasma cutting | Extended reach (1900mm), designed for both arc welding and plasma cutting. Integrated cables reduce damage risk |
| **Motoman AR900 or AR700** | Tighter welding cells | Shorter reach for compact sub-assembly welding of smaller "kiddie parts" |
| **ArcWorld 6000-series cells** | Complete welding workstations | Pre-engineered cells with positioners, safety fencing, and integrated welding power supplies |

#### Secondary Possibility: Kawasaki Robotics

**Evidence:**
- Kawasaki Robotics has a documented relationship with Suzuki globally (Maruti Suzuki training in India)
- Kawasaki's own motorcycle factory (KMM in Lincoln, NE) uses 71+ Kawasaki arc welding robots for ATV and four-wheeler frame welding - the exact same application
- Kawasaki is Japanese, fitting the Japanese manufacturing philosophy
- **However:** There is no identified Kawasaki robotics service partner local to Rome, GA, which weakens this compared to the Yaskawa/ADF connection

#### Tertiary Possibility: Fanuc

**Evidence:**
- ADF Welding also programs Fanuc robots
- Fanuc dominates global industrial robotics market share
- Fanuc is Japanese
- **However:** The local service infrastructure points more strongly to Yaskawa Motoman

## TDK Sensor Implementation - Revised Analysis

### Correction from Initial Assessment

My initial analysis suggested TDK's Tronics MEMS vibration sensors as a likely product. **This is probably wrong.** The Tronics vibration sensor line (VIBO platform) was announced entering the market in **September 2025** and was still "completing development of its first generation." If SMAC implemented TDK sensors 6-7 months ago (approximately mid-2025), the Tronics VIBO sensors would not have been commercially available yet.

### What They Almost Certainly Deployed: TDK i3 Micro CbM Sensor System

The **TDK SensEI i3 Micro Module** is the right product. Here's why:

**Timeline fit:** The i3 Micro Module was announced at CES 2023, won Product of the Year 2023, and has been commercially available since. A mid-2025 implementation timeline aligns perfectly.

**Product fit for SMAC's needs:**
- Battery-powered, wireless, no wiring needed (critical for retrofitting onto existing robots without downtime)
- IP54 dustproof/waterproof (essential in a welding shop with sparks, heat, and metal dust)
- Mesh networking up to 100 sensors (enough to cover all robots + key equipment)
- Edge AI processes data locally (reduced network infrastructure requirements)
- No external network required (important for manufacturing security/confidentiality)
- Compact: 56 x 41 x 20mm (can mount on robot arm joints without interference)

### Technical Specifications of the i3 Micro CbM System

| Component | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Sensor** | TDK IIM-42352 SmartIndustrial MEMS accelerometer |
| **Axes** | 3-axis (X, Y, Z) vibration detection |
| **Accelerometer Range** | Programmable: +/-2g, +/-4g, +/-8g, +/-16g |
| **Shock Tolerance** | 20,000g |
| **Processor** | TI CC2652R7 Arm Cortex-M4F (32-bit wireless MCU) |
| **Connectivity** | BLE + wireless mesh network (2.4 GHz ISM band) |
| **Network Capacity** | Up to 100 sensor modules per mesh |
| **Size** | 56 x 41 x 20 mm |
| **Power** | CR2477 coin cell battery |
| **Protection** | IP54 (dustproof / waterproof) |
| **AI** | Built-in edge AI for anomaly detection |
| **Software** | CbM Studio PC application (data collection, analysis, AI model training) |
| **Gateway** | Network Controller (USB interface to PC/host) |
| **Compliance** | FCC Part 15, RoHS |

### The Full System Deployment

The i3 CbM Solution is a **3-component system**:

1. **i3 Micro Modules** (the sensors themselves) - small battery-powered units physically mounted on equipment
2. **Network Controller** (gateway) - a dedicated wireless gateway that connects to a PC/host via USB and manages the mesh network
3. **CbM Studio Software** (PC application) - handles data collection, visualization, AI model training, and anomaly alerting

### What This Looks Like on SMAC's Robots

**Physical installation:**
- Small white/gray sensor modules (about the size of a matchbox) magnetically or adhesive-mounted on:
  - Robot arm joints (J1-J6 axes) - especially J2, J3, J4 where bearing loads are highest
  - Robot base/pedestal
  - Welding positioner bearings
  - Plasma torch head assemblies
  - Hydraulic press bearings and actuators
  - Conveyor drive motors
- Each module has a small LED indicator for status
- No wires running to or from the sensors - they're completely wireless

**How they form the network:**
- When powered on, the i3 Micro Modules automatically discover each other and form a wireless mesh network
- The mesh self-heals if a node goes down or is temporarily blocked (common in a metal-heavy factory environment)
- Data hops through the mesh to reach the Network Controller, which is connected via USB to a monitoring PC/workstation
- A single Network Controller can manage up to 100 sensors

**What the monitoring looks like:**
- CbM Studio running on a PC/workstation (likely in the maintenance office or on the factory floor)
- Real-time vibration data visualization for each sensor
- AI model training: the system learns the "normal" vibration signature of each robot/piece of equipment during healthy operation
- When vibration patterns deviate from learned baselines, the system flags anomalies
- Indicators like Peak, Peak-Peak, RMS, and Crest Factor are calculated on-device (edge AI)
- Only processed results are transmitted - raw data stays on the module, conserving bandwidth and battery

### What SMAC Likely Wants From This (Use Cases)

Given the nature of their equipment (welding robots, plasma cutting robots, hydraulic presses, conveyor systems), the TDK sensors are almost certainly being used for:

#### 1. Welding Robot Joint/Bearing Degradation Detection
**The #1 use case.** Arc welding robots perform tens of thousands of repetitive high-precision motions per day. Over time:
- Joint bearings wear (especially J2 and J3 which bear the most load)
- Gearbox backlash increases
- Motor performance degrades
- Cable routing through the arm can degrade

The i3 sensors detect changes in vibration signature that indicate bearing wear, misalignment, or gear degradation **weeks before failure**. This is critical because a single robot going down on a line producing 120 ATVs/day costs real money.

#### 2. Plasma Cutting Robot Health Monitoring
Similar to welding robots but with additional thermal stress from the plasma process. The sensor's temperature monitoring capability (via the accelerometer's built-in temperature sensor) adds value here.

#### 3. Hydraulic Press Monitoring
Hydraulic presses have known failure modes (bearing wear, hydraulic pump degradation, cylinder seal failure) that produce distinct vibration signatures detectable by MEMS accelerometers.

#### 4. Conveyor System Health
The automated e-coat and powder-coat conveyor systems have motors, bearings, chains, and sprockets that benefit from vibration monitoring.

#### 5. Injection Molding Machine Monitoring
The 3,300-ton injection molding machine has high-force actuators and bearings that are prime candidates for condition-based monitoring.

### What This Replaces

Before the TDK sensors, SMAC likely relied on:
- **Calendar-based preventive maintenance** (replace bearings every X months regardless of condition)
- **Manual vibration checks** (maintenance tech with a handheld vibration meter doing spot checks)
- **Run-to-failure** on lower-priority equipment
- **Operator judgment** ("that robot sounds different today")

The TDK i3 system shifts them toward **condition-based maintenance (CbM)** - maintaining equipment based on actual measured condition rather than calendar schedules. This means:
- Fewer unexpected breakdowns
- Less over-maintenance (replacing parts that still have life)
- Better spare parts planning
- Higher overall equipment effectiveness (OEE)

### Integration with CMMS (MaintainX)

If SMAC uses MaintainX as their CMMS, the TDK system would feed into it through:
- Maintenance technicians creating work orders in MaintainX when CbM Studio flags an anomaly
- Potentially automated work order creation via API integration
- Meter readings from the sensors being logged against robot assets
- Procedure templates for sensor-triggered maintenance tasks
